In short

Your rating is on the familiar NTRP scale (2.5, 3.0, 3.5 …), shown to two decimals so you can see small changes. After every match that counts, it moves up if your side won more games than expected, and down if it won fewer. It's not about who won the match: losing 4-6 to a much stronger side can still raise your rating. Other players can't see your rating.

Where you start

Everyone starts at their level in the club's Members table, which the developers keep. If the club changes your level there, your rating is worked out again from that new starting point. Your rating is provisional for your first 5 results, while it moves faster to find your level.

Which results count

  • Sunday Doubles: the scores in your Game Review. Each group of sets with the same partners counts as one result.
  • Other games with members, casual play or league games, reported on the Report a game tab, from the last 30 days. The player reporting it gives the day, singles or doubles, casual play or which league, the players and the score.

Every score is checked by the other players:

  • When someone reports a game you played in, you're asked to Confirm or Dispute it the next time you open My Rating, before you can carry on.
  • Reported games count only once every other player in the game, your partner included, has confirmed the score.
  • Sunday Doubles: one opponent confirming is enough. If an opponent's own Game Review has the same score, that confirms it. A Sunday result nobody answers counts automatically 7 days after its latest score was given.
  • Disputing a score means giving the score you think is right (and, if you like, why). Your score then goes back to the other players, including whoever reported it, to confirm or dispute in turn. It goes back and forth like this until everyone has confirmed the same score, and only then counts. Everyone sees each score that was proposed. A Game Review with a different score works the same way.
  • A score can be disputed up to 3 times. The 3rd dispute sends the game to the developers, who settle the score (or decide the game doesn't count), with every score proposed in front of them.
  • Ratings are worked out again from all counted results each time, in date order, so a late confirmation or a corrected score fixes everything after it.

How one result changes your rating

change = step size × how much it counts × (share of games you won − share you were expected to win)

Share of games you won. All the games in the match: winning 6-3, 6-4 is 12 of 19 games, 63%.

Share you were expected to win comes from the gap between the two sides' ratings before the match. In doubles, a side's rating is the average of the two partners. Every 0.5 of a gap doubles the expected odds:

How much it counts. A match of 20 games or more counts in full. Shorter ones count in proportion, so a single 6-4 set (10 games) counts half.

An example. You're rated 3.50 with 10 results so far. You and your partner (3.50) beat two 3.50 players 6-3, 6-4. Expected: 50% (an even match). Won: 12 of 19 = 63%. Counts: 19 of 20 games = 95%. Change = 0.27 × 0.95 × (63% − 50%) = +0.034, so your rating goes to 3.53. Your partner moves by the same result, with their own step size; your opponents move down the same way.

Ratings stay between 1.0 and 7.0. On My Rating, “How this was worked out” under each result shows these numbers, and the What if? box lets you try any result.

Your level, and the 0.1 buffer

Your level is your rating rounded to the nearest 0.5: a rating of 3.15 is a 3.0, and 3.9 is a 4.0. The halfway points between levels are at x.25 and x.75. (USTA rounds up instead: their 3.5 players are rated 3.01 to 3.50.)

So that a rating sitting right on a halfway point doesn't flip your level back and forth every week, your level only changes once your rating is 0.1 past the halfway point:

  • Up: a 3.5 becomes a 4.0 when the rating reaches 3.85 (not 3.75).
  • Down: a 3.5 becomes a 3.0 when the rating falls below 3.15 (not 3.25).

For example, a 3.5 player whose rating dips to 3.20 stays a 3.5; at 3.12 they become a 3.0, and then need to reach 3.35 to be a 3.5 again.

Your level is also what the Sunday Doubles organizers use to put players on courts.

Who sees what

You see your rating and its history. Other players never see it. The club's developers (the volunteers who run the app) can look up any player's rating and its history, the same chart and table you see, to check that ratings work fairly and to answer questions about them. They also see your level (in 0.5 steps, e.g. 3.5) in the club's Members table, where the Sunday Doubles scheduler uses it, and the games and scores that were reported, and who has confirmed them, for example to follow a league's results and to settle disputes. The share of games you were expected to win is shown so you can check the maths, so it does hint at your opponents' ratings at the time.

Sources

The method combines well-known, published rating ideas. The particular numbers (0.5 of a gap doubles the odds, the 0.8 in the step size, 20 games to count in full, the 0.1 buffer) are the club's choices, and may be tuned.

  1. Expected share from the rating gap: the Bradley–Terry model for paired comparisons, where each fixed gap multiplies the odds by the same amount. R. A. Bradley and M. E. Terry (1952), “Rank analysis of incomplete block designs: I. The method of paired comparisons”, Biometrika 39(3/4), 324–345, doi:10.2307/2334029. Overview: Bradley–Terry model (Wikipedia).
  2. Moving the rating by result minus expectation: the Elo rating system, used for chess and many sports. Elo rating system (Wikipedia). The chess federation's rules give new players bigger steps (K = 40 until 30 games, then 20): FIDE Rating Regulations, section 8.5.
  3. Step size shrinking with results played, 1 ÷ (results + 5)0.4: from Elo ratings for tennis, which predicted professional matches best among the models compared. S. Kovalchik (2016), “Searching for the GOAT of tennis win prediction”, Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports 12(3), 127–138, doi:10.1515/jqas-2015-0059.
  4. Counting games won rather than wins, and weighting longer matches more: the Universal Tennis Rating (UTR) compares the games you actually win with the games expected from the rating gap, and gives longer formats more weight. How UTR Rating Works (UTR Sports).
  5. The 0.5 levels: the USTA's National Tennis Rating Program (NTRP). Understanding NTRP Ratings (USTA).
